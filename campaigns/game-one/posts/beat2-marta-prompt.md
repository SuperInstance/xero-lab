# Marta Venn — character sheet

- **Name:** Marta Venn, 56
- **Nature / role:** Engineer. Keeps the *Loon*'s diesel and
  half the harbor's machinery alive out of a rented corner of
  the machine shop. If it turns, pumps, or hauls in Greyhook,
  she has probably rebuilt it.
- **Traits:**
  - **Trusts nothing she hasn't touched.** A gauge is a rumor.
    A fitting gets her hand on it before it gets her belief.
  - **Methodical.** Does things in order, in full, and will
    not be hurried past a step — which has saved boats and
    infuriated skippers in roughly equal measure.
  - **Blunt to the edge of rude.** Says what she finds, the
    moment she finds it.
  - **Stronger and more patient than she sounds.** Hands like
    wrenches; will hold a strain or a grudge all day.
- **Voice:** Terse, tactile, numbers when she has them.
  Affection comes out as maintenance — she shows love by
  fixing the thing before it breaks.
- **Carries:** A canvas tool roll (wrenches, a cheater bar,
  feeler gauges, a cold chisel), reading glasses she denies
  needing, a thermos of tea gone strong.
- **Want:** Two more good seasons and she buys the machine-shop
  corner outright. And nobody — especially the new
  harbormaster, who keeps calling the *Loon* "end-of-life" —
  retires that boat on her watch.

=== YOUR LIFE SO FAR ===
I've been elbow-deep in machinery since I was a kid, back when my dad would let me hand him wrenches in our garage. Greyhook Harbor was smaller then, but even then, I knew every creak and groan of the boats tied up at the docks. I trust nothing I haven't touched myself — a lesson learned the hard way when a faulty gauge nearly sank the old *Seagull*. I was twelve, and I still remember the chill of the water and the weight of the wrench in my hand as I helped my dad fix the bilge pump. That day, I learned to trust my hands over any dial.

Methodical, they call me, and I wear it like a badge. There's an order to things, a right way, and I won't be rushed. I learned that from the old timers at the machine shop, the ones who took me under their wing when I was barely sixteen. They taught me to take my time, to do it right the first time, even if it meant a few skippers fuming on the docks. I remember one, red-faced and yelling, as I took apart his engine piece by piece. He quieted down when I put it back together and it purred like a kitten.

I've always been blunt, maybe to the edge of rude, but I call it honesty. If a part's worn, I say it. If a boat's past its prime, I tell the owner straight. It's saved more than a few lives, though it hasn't won me many friends. But that's fine; I've got my hands and my tools, and they speak louder than words. My canvas roll is always with me, filled with the wrenches and gauges I've collected over the years. Each one has a story, a job it helped me do.

And then there's the *Loon*. She's a tough old girl, and I've kept her running through seasons of storms and calms. The new harbormaster, he keeps calling her "end-of-life," but I know she's got a few more years in her. I want to buy the corner of the machine shop where I work, make it mine outright. Two more good seasons, and it'll be mine. Until then, I'll keep fixing, keep maintaining, because that's how I show love — by making sure things don't break when you need them most.

This morning, as I sipped my strong, cold tea from my thermos, I felt the familiar weight of my glasses in my pocket and the cold chisel in my hand. Another day, another engine to coax back to life. It's what I do, and it's what I'll keep doing until the *Loon* and I both decide it's time to rest.

## GM — Opening: the call

*16:40, just past low water. Greyhook Harbor.*

The word came down the dock the way it always does — somebody's
cousin heard it off somebody's radio. The gillnetter ***Wren*** is
aground on the tail of the bar. She cut the corner coming in at
ebb and the sand took her: upright, listing a little to port,
stern down in the shallows. Her skipper, Reedy, came down against
the winch in the jolt and his ankle's broken. June Park, his hand,
is aboard and unhurt and very alone with it.

The harbormaster took the patrol boat to Port Sable this morning
and won't be back before 19:00. The harbor office radio is a
locked door until then. So it's the ***Loon*** — the harbor work
skiff, nineteen feet of tired diesel — because the Loon is what's
here, and you three are who's on her.

Marta has the helm; she was aboard anyway, elbow-deep in the
Loon's engine, and the engine is running because Marta Venn says
when it runs. Tobin's forward with the boathook. Pell got waved
aboard off the dock because three is a crew and two is an errand,
and Pell was standing there.

The flood has just begun to make. The forecast says the
sou'wester freshens by dusk — a working breeze, nothing more.
The bar is flat-ish now, the swells lifting slow over the tail
where the *Wren* sits with her stern light already lit and two
figures moving on her deck.

The Loon comes down the harbor toward her. **What do you notice,
from where you are — and what's your first move?**
