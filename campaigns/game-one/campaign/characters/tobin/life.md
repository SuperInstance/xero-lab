I was born with salt in my veins, or that's what my granddad used to say. Greyhook Harbor is all I've ever known, the creak of the docks and the slap of waves against the hulls as familiar as my own heartbeat. My left knee's been a barometer since I was fifteen, when a sudden squall caught us off guard and a swinging mast caught me instead. Laid up for weeks, I learned to read the ache like the old salts read the clouds. It's never steered me wrong.

Growing up around here, you either sink or swim, and I learned to swim before I could walk. The water's never scared me, not even when the surf's churning and the cold bites through to your bones. It's where I feel most alive, most myself. I remember my first real swim, out past the breakwater and back, my granddad watching from the shore, his old brass compass in his hand. He gave it to me after that, said I'd earned it. It's not the fanciest, but it works, and it's mine.

I've been working the docks since I was old enough to haul a net, and now I'm the youngest hand on the *Loon*. They call me "the kid," but I know my way around a boat better than most. I've volunteered for every wet, dirty job there is, sometimes before anyone even asks. I guess you could say I'm hungry to prove myself, to be more than just "the kid." I want to be a real hand, to be trusted with more than just the grunt work. I want to earn my place, not just be given it.

So here I am, nineteen years old, with a knife on my belt, oilskins in my duffel, and my granddad's compass in my pocket. The weather's changing, I can feel it in my bones, and I'm ready for whatever comes next.
