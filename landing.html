Start with a blank page.
It's already an agent.

Xero doesn't start with a harness and fit an agent inside it. It starts with a blank page at limitless potential — already an agent, with no name yet. Everything after that is an addition: a plug-in layer that spends some of that potential to buy one definite capability. The first addition is the most expensive decision in the system — it's the model of understanding everything else gets built on.

NOT ALIGNMENT

Orientation comes first

A big model is gunpowder — powerful, directionless, needing a barrel. Xero is before gunpowder: potential with nothing to contain yet. It doesn't need aligning to a heading. It needs orienting from the right place, once, before the first layer goes on.

EVERY ADDITION HAS A PRICE

Potential spent, capability bought

Each plug-in is a constraint. The question is never “what belongs in Xero?” — it's “which constraints are worth the potential they cost, and in what order?”

THE FUEL IS MINOR

The system does the work

At the end of the chain, the model is the burning — minor. The engine around it is what's giving you the lighting and the heating. Xero builds the engine out of small, definite pieces.

Where it's just perfect

Eight situations where starting from a blank page — and adding only what the job earns — beats starting from a harness. Every demo below runs in your browser. Play with them.

● Real prototype

1 · The walking dictation

You're walking for twenty minutes, thinking out loud, with long silences while you look out the window. A normal voice agent answers at the first pause and makes you feed it words. Xero's voice block just listens — transcribing to markdown chunks that fall out at the pauses: emit at 60 seconds, at ¾ of a page, or after a gap over 5 seconds. One block, three cadences — and a toggle between them.

Patient holds through the silences. On the tested 19-minute sparse walk, Patient answered twice and held 499 words.

The numbers are from the prototype run: on the same 19-minute sparse walk, Eager answered 45 times, Patient 2 times, and Async surfaced 9 nudge points — finishing all 9 jobs before the speaker stopped, with its first response at 64s instead of 169s thanks to a wall-clock tick in the silences. The segmenter ended mid-sentence 0% of the time; a naive fixed-window splitter did it 82–100% of the time. No major voice product ships this toggle.

2 · The 3 a.m. bilge

A bilge level rises and falls all night. Inside the deadband, that's just the boat breathing — silence. Outside it, something is wrong, and the agent says so. Once.

3 · The spreadsheet that fires its teacher

Put the work in cells. The big model's job is the cut — breaking work into decisions small enough for little models to make. Judgment sets the gate on each one: go, hold, or stop.

Decision grid · amber = big model · green = small model · violet = heldAll cells on the big model
Cost per pass100%
8.3%Of all-big cost — staffed grid
0 / 395Family cells kept by the big model

Measured on the real run — 395 repos × 4 decision cells. A naming rule staffs 85.8% of cells; the big model keeps just 8%. And the gates belong on instances, not blanket rules: gating individual decisions, Jev accepted 11 of 11 correct small-model calls and 0 of 18 wrong ones — the deadband caught the rest. The teacher works itself out of a job, cell by cell — and every verdict is logged. Nobody grades their own work: the decider doesn't set its own gate.
Honest caveat: the small-model tier ran partial — a rate-limit excursion stopped it mid-run, recorded in the sheet itself. A paced re-run is queued.

● Measured — reader tuning is next

4 · The 20° turn, read in letters

Point the same widget at the sea. A camera watches the waves; the sea is rendered as letters — character by height, resolution by motion. A model tuned to read waveforms as a first-class language pulls the gist — rhythm, height, throw — straight from the lettering. Now turn 20° into the wind: the general rules price the wind, but the letter-sea supplies the subtlety, because the camera sees the swell before the hull feels it.

Letter-sea · liveSets & lulls · swell from the NW

➤Holding course. Rules alone would add throttle for the wind.

Measured — 36 synthetic seas read under four encodings. Compactness confirmed: letters cost 6.5× fewer tokens per reading than numeric grids and lost nothing to them on any question. One surprise: per-cell delta encoding loses on wave fields — everything moves a little, all the time — so plain frames plus the deadband idea win. The honest caveat is the reader, not the rendering: zero-shot, an untuned model reads the sea poorly in every encoding — it even insists every sea is “building.” The information doesn't die in the letters; it dies in an untrained reader. Next step: tune a small reader on the generator's labeled seas — Jev's verdict on that plan: 0.73, yes. Then you prune away everything the reader doesn't need.

5 · The combadge asks the smallest brain that can hold the question

A pin at the edge, a brain on the boat's network, a big brain in the cloud. Ask something small and it never leaves the pin. Ask something big and watch it travel.

Placement · reflex → LAN → cloudIdle
◉ PINon the metal · reflex
▣ LAN BRAINlocal GPU · deliberation
☁ CLOUDbig model · the cut

The negotiation rule: smallest brain that can hold the question. Reflex on the metal, deliberation nearby, the heavy cut in the cloud — one loop, three addresses.

This pattern already has fleet receipts: an edge observer feeding context packets to a voice agent that synthesizes the spoken reply. Local when home, cloud when not — layered competence, graceful degradation.

7 · The redshirt comes home

Send a minimal copy out disconnected — a desert-island deployment. It works offline inside its own complete little reality and learns things. When it comes home, it does not trust that reality. It checks its perceptions against the fleet first.

On return · perception checkToggle a view

Sync is not copying files. Sync is a perception check.

Where the copy and the fleet disagree, the difference arrives as an excursion — a note, not a silent overwrite. The copy re-syncs knowing exactly where its little reality was wrong, and where it was right first.

● Simulation · measured in the fleet

8 · Two clones, one bank account of truth

Two copies work the same job in parallel and write records as they go. Neither copy's state is stored anywhere. State is derived — folded from the record, the way a bank balance is folded from the ledger. Disagree? The record decides.

Arrival order vs. folded stateReady
Records: A · B · C · D · E · F
Folded state → balance 42 · jobs done 6 · open item: “seal the hatch”
Shuffles: 0 · Different outcomes: 0

Measured, not asserted: in the fleet this was tested directly — 50 shuffled arrival orders produced identical state. The record is primary; state is derived. That's an orientation, not a feature: the agent is the story, and the story is append-only.

The library behind the page

Xero wasn't drawn on a whiteboard. It was found — underneath a year and a half of building, in a library big enough that the usual tools silently capped out before seeing all of it.

5,231public repos on the SuperInstance account — enumerated page by page and mapped in full
41families of 12+ repos hold 2,874 of them; every repo accounted for exactly once, down to 938 singletons
10research files in the library — hardware, history, and constraints, with sources and honest gaps

The research confirmed the hardware ladder's bottom rung: the Arduino UNO Q — a dual-brain board, Linux on one side and a real-time microcontroller on the other — reflex on the metal, deliberation on Linux, radios to reach out. Alongside it: PLATO/TUTOR, MUD room architecture, Telegram's bot constraints, and the survey's rarest catch — the forgotten lowest-level pieces that pass the removal test. Balanced ternary, the library's biggest bet at 371 repos, scored 0.06 and failed it: size is not depth.
